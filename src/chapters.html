Chapter I.

The Burial Question

London, 1840–1852

London’s churchyards full

By the 1840s London had run out of room for its dead. Its churchyards and small private burial grounds were full to the point of public scandal, and the new joint-stock garden cemeteries ringing the capital, Kensal Green, Highgate, Abney Park, Brompton and the rest, could not keep pace with a population that was doubling within a lifetime. Evidence gathered in the 1840s showed that Spa Fields in Clerkenwell, a ground meant for perhaps 1,000 bodies, held some 80,000, and that gravediggers broke up older remains to make room for new.

Cholera, 1848–49

Cholera turned a nuisance into an emergency. The epidemic of 1848–49 killed between 14,600 and 14,800 Londoners, and most sanitary reformers still believed that disease rose as a miasma from rotting matter, graveyards included. Edwin Chadwick, a commissioner of the General Board of Health, proposed closing the existing burial grounds near London and burying the capital’s dead in two large cemeteries just outside the Metropolitan Burial District.

The State tries, and withdraws

Parliament first tried to do the job itself. The Metropolitan Interments Act of 5 August 1850 made London a single burial district under the Board of Health, with power to buy land and existing cemeteries, open new ones, and recommend the closure of old grounds. The scheme was widely unpopular and the Treasury doubted it could pay: only one cemetery, now Brompton, was ever bought under the Act. The Burial Act of 1 July 1852 repealed it and put a different mechanism in its place: individual burial grounds could now be closed by Order in Council, and parishes could form burial boards to provide new ones. Closures followed in quick succession, and the dead of central London needed somewhere to go.

Broun and Sprye’s scheme, 1849–50

Two promoters, Sir Richard Broun and Richard Sprye, had already seen the opportunity. In 1849–50 they proposed one vast cemetery on cheap heathland near Woking, some 37 km (23 miles) from London, served by its own funeral trains over the London and South Western Railway, whose main line, as the London and Southampton Railway, had reached Woking in 1838. A train could carry the dead further, faster and more cheaply than the slow horse-drawn hearses that served the existing cemeteries, and the distance answered the sanitary objection.

The founders bought out

The idea outlived its authors’ control of it. In April 1851 a group of trustees led by William Voules bought Broun and Sprye’s rights for £20,000 and, once the Act had passed, founded a company without regard to their agreement with the original promoters. Its private Act, the London Necropolis and National Mausoleum Act (15 & 16 Vict. c. cxlix), received Royal Assent on 30 June 1852. The “National Mausoleum” of the title, a vast mausoleum church promised in Broun’s prospectus, was never built.

The Onslow heath

Under its Act the company bought common land at Woking from Lord Onslow: 2,268 acres according to Historic England, about 2,000 to 2,200 in rounder accounts. It was poor, sandy heath, worth little for farming, and far more than a cemetery could ever use. The company laid out only some 350–500 acres at the western end, where there was then no settlement, for Brookwood village grew up only from the 1880s; the rest of the estate became its land bank.

A boardroom in crisis

The company’s early life was quarrelsome. In early 1853, amid allegations of voting irregularities and with the promised dividends unpaid, several directors resigned, Voules among them, and public confidence in the scheme collapsed. After a committee of enquiry and a reform of the board in September 1853, work on the ground began.

Smirke, Thomas and Donald

In 1853 Sydney Smirke, architect of the British Museum’s Reading Room, replaced the company’s first architect, Henry Abraham, and designed the two chapels and two stations inside the cemetery. Historic England says Smirke was “apparently” also responsible, with William Broderick Thomas, for the detailed landscaping, laid out on the principles of J. C. Loudon, the great theorist of the garden cemetery, and that the planting was carried out by the local nurseryman Robert Donald, of Goldsworth, Woking, an associate of Loudon’s. The attribution of the landscaping to William Tite, sometimes repeated, rests on little more than a suggestion that he advised Smirke and planned the planting of the branch line; Tite is firmly documented as architect of the London terminus.

Wellingtonias and rhododendrons

Donald’s planting was meant to give the cemetery an atmosphere of perpetual spring. Seed of the newly introduced American conifers was reaching Britain just as the cemetery opened, and Brookwood took them up with enthusiasm: giant sequoias, known in England as Wellingtonias, monkey puzzles and redwoods, set among rhododendrons that the company often planted to give community plots privacy and seclusion. The mature Wellingtonias lining Long Avenue are the cemetery’s signature.

Church and Chapel divided

The ground was split by religion as well as by class. The southern part was consecrated for the Church of England; the northern part was left unconsecrated for Nonconformists and, as it turned out, for every other faith. Each half had its own chapel and its own railway station. That unconsecrated northern ground, which the Burial Act of 1852 required of new cemeteries, gave room to other communities: Swedes, Catholics and Zoroastrians were the first groups outside the Church of England to take plots, and many more followed.

7 and 13 November 1854

Charles Sumner, Bishop of Winchester, consecrated the southern half, then called the Woking Necropolis, on 7 November 1854. The first funeral train ran from London on 13 November, and the first burials followed the same day. The first entry in the register is the stillborn twin sons of Mr and Mrs Hore of Ewer Street, Borough, buried at the expense of the parish of St Saviour’s, Southwark. Almost all of the 43 burials in that first month were, in John Clarke’s judgement, pauper funerals, and none of the graves can be identified today. The first permanent memorial was raised to Sir Henry Goldfinch, who died on 21 November 1854.

Selling the Heath

Land sales keep the company afloat

The dead never came in the numbers the promoters had promised. They had spoken of 10,000 or more burials a year; Brookwood never handled more than about 4,500. The company survived by selling the land it did not need. In 1858 it sold 64 acres to the Government for the Woking Invalid Convict Prison. Around 150 acres went for the second Surrey County Asylum, later Brookwood Hospital. Ten acres on Maybury Common were sold for £750 to the Royal Dramatic College, a home for retired actors; when it failed, its buildings passed to Dr Gottlieb Leitner for his Oriental Institute, beside which the Shah Jahan Mosque was built in 1889. In 1878, through a third party, for reasons the lost records do not explain, the company sold about an acre at St John’s to the Cremation Society, where Britain’s first purpose-built crematorium carried out its first official cremation on 26 March 1885. The modern town of Woking grew up around the station from the mid-1860s, as the company sold off surplus land for development.

Chapter III.

Decline and Revival

1941 to the present day

After the railway

Without its railway, and with too few burials to pay for the upkeep of so large a site, the necropolis had lean post-war decades. Undergrowth and rhododendron spread over the older plots, buildings decayed, and the South station, by then a storeroom, burned in September 1972. The London Necropolis Company was taken over in 1959 and passed through a succession of owners; one of them, Maximillian Investments, which bought it in 1973, obtained the Brookwood Cemetery Act of 1975 to sell off land. Most of its records were destroyed when its offices moved to a small former caretaker’s lodge.

Güney, 1985–2006

In 1985 Ramadan Güney bought the cemetery from its then owner; the purchase grew out of his chairmanship of the UK Turkish Islamic Trust, which wanted burial facilities for its members. He died in Northern Cyprus on 2 November 2006 and was buried in his family vault at Brookwood on 10 November. His son ran the cemetery until, in 2011–12, the courts upheld a challenge to the inheritance by Güney’s former partner, who sold it to Woking Borough Council in 2014.

Woking Borough Council, 2014

In December 2014 Woking Borough Council bought the companies that owned the civilian cemetery, Brookwood Park Limited and Brookwood Cemetery Limited, on confidential terms. The war graves were never part of the sale. Brookwood has also been chosen as the reburial ground for remains excavated by HS2 from St James’s Gardens, Euston, an echo of the clearances of Victorian London.

Grade I; the Society

Historic England registered the cemetery at Grade I on the Register of Parks and Gardens on 25 January 1993, describing it as the largest cemetery in England, and many individual monuments are listed. It remains on the Heritage at Risk register, its condition “generally satisfactory but with significant localised problems”. The Brookwood Cemetery Society, a voluntary group founded in 1992, leads walks, holds open days, publishes The Brookwood Express and has restored, or helped to restore, neglected memorials, among them Mangles’s in 2006 and Bradlaugh’s in 2021, when a replica bust replaced the one stolen some years before.

Chapter IV.

The Necropolis Railway

Westminster Bridge Road to Brookwood, 1854–1941

A private terminus at Waterloo

The railway was the whole premise of the scheme. The company built its own London terminus beside Waterloo, between Westminster Bridge Road and York Street (now Leake Street), designed by William Tite with William Cubitt as engineer. Funeral trains ran from there over the London and South Western main line and turned off, just west of Brookwood, on to the company’s private branch into the cemetery.

121 Westminster Bridge Road, 1902

When the railway needed the site to enlarge Waterloo, it built the company a replacement across the road at 121 Westminster Bridge Road, which opened in 1902 (8 February, by the usual account). Its office block survives as Westminster Bridge House, and the first-class entrance can still be seen in the façade.

Class and creed, even in death

The trains kept the distinctions of Victorian society intact to the graveside. Coffins travelled on First, Second or Third Class tickets (“one-way, of course”, in the phrase of the railway’s historian, John Clarke), and mourners travelled in the class of funeral they had paid for. Third Class was almost always used for paupers buried at the expense of their parish. Anglicans and Nonconformists, living and dead, were kept apart all the way to their separate stations. Traffic

At first a train ran once a day, whenever funerals were booked. Sunday trains ended after October 1900, and by the 1930s it was unusual for one to run more than twice a week. Brookwood recorded 203,041 burials between 1854 and 1941, though not every coffin came by rail. The line also carried the contents of whole London burial grounds: in 1862 the building of Charing Cross station and its approaches cleared the Cure’s College ground in Southwark, and at least 7,950 bodies went to Brookwood, the first of many such removals.

Nicknames

Railwaymen had their own names for the trains. John Clarke records “the stiffs” and “the dead meat train”.

16–17 April 1941

The last funeral train left Westminster Bridge Road on 11 April 1941, reportedly carrying the Chelsea Pensioner Edward Irish. Five nights later, on 16–17 April, an air raid burned the rolling stock in the siding and destroyed much of the terminus. After the war the directors judged the private train obsolete; from 1946 funerals travelled by ordinary Southern Railway services to Brookwood’s main-line station, and the track inside the cemetery was lifted around 1947–48. The North station was later demolished, and the South station burned in 1972. Its platform survives beside the St Edward Brotherhood’s monastery, and the old trackbed is now Railway Avenue.

Chapter VIII.

Parishes, Guilds and Faiths

The grounds within the ground

London parishes

The company sold land wholesale as well as grave by grave. London parishes bought grounds for their dead: St Saviour’s, Southwark, from the first day until about 1900. Others, among them Bermondsey from December 1854, used Brookwood without a known plot of their own. Friendly societies, trades and charities followed, and the historian John Clarke has counted more than sixty organisations that used Brookwood.

Actors, Oddfellows, Commissionaires

In 1857 or 1858 the actor John Anson acquired about an acre for the Dramatic, Equestrian and Musical Sick Fund Association, the beginning of Actors’ Acre. The Oddfellows of the Metropolitan District reserved a plot in 1859 and raised a central memorial in 1861; the Ancient Order of Foresters followed in the 1860s. The Corps of Commissionaires, founded in 1859 to employ ex-servicemen, buried many of its members and their families here, two of them holders of the Victoria Cross. From 1893 or 1894 the Royal Hospital Chelsea brought its in-pensioners to Brookwood.

The unconsecrated north

The northern ground, left unconsecrated for Nonconformists, proved hospitable to faiths the founders can hardly have imagined. Brookwood’s Catholic (1859), Swedish (1857), Parsi (1862) and Muslim grounds were all laid out there.

The Zoroastrian Ground

1862

London’s Parsi community opened its burial ground in the north-west of the cemetery in 1862, a year after it founded its religious trust fund; it is described as the only Zoroastrian burial ground in Europe. Its mausolea are among the most remarkable in the cemetery. That of Nowrosjee Nusserwanjee Wadia (d. 1899) is modelled on the tomb of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae. Three Tata mausolea stand together to the left of the entrance, the central one built for Jamsetji Tata, founder of the Tata group, with those of his sons Sir Ratanji and Sir Dorabji beside it. Two of the early British MPs of Indian descent lie here too: Sir Mancherjee Bhownaggree, Conservative, and Shapurji Saklatvala, Communist.

The Muslim Grounds

1884: the first in Britain

In 1884 a plot of 27 feet by 24 feet was set aside for Dr Gottlieb Leitner’s Oriental Institute at Woking. Marked by a stone giving the direction of prayer, it is described as the first Muslim burial ground in Britain. The cemetery’s records counted 390 Muslim burials by 1949. In the twentieth century prominent converts and scholars of British Islam were buried here: Lord Headley, the Irish peer who took the name Shaikh Rahmatullah al-Farooq; Marmaduke Pickthall and Abdullah Yusuf Ali, whose translations of the Qur’an are still read across the English-speaking world; and, in an unmarked grave, Abdullah Quilliam, founder of the Liverpool Muslim Institute.

Brookwood today

The Muslim presence has since become the cemetery’s mainstay. In 1985 the Turkish Cypriot businessman Ramadan Güney bought the whole cemetery, a purchase that grew out of his chairmanship of the UK Turkish Islamic Trust, which wanted burial facilities for its members. In recent years more than seven in ten burials at Brookwood have been Muslim, in separate grounds for Shia, Ismaili, Dawoodi Bohra, Ahmadiyya, Memon and other communities. Among them lie the Palestinian cartoonist Naji al-Ali, murdered in London in 1987, and the architect Dame Zaha Hadid, buried in 2016 between her father and her brother.

The Orthodox, the Swedes and the Catholics

St Edward the Martyr

Monks of the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia arrived in March 1982 and founded the St Edward Brotherhood in the former Anglican chapels near the old South station. In September 1984 they enshrined relics identified as those of St Edward the Martyr, the Anglo-Saxon king murdered at Corfe in 978, while a legal dispute over who owned them was still unresolved.

Solander; a Fenian

The Swedish Church has its own ground, to which the coffin of Daniel Solander, the botanist who sailed with Banks and Cook in the Endeavour, was moved from Wapping in the early twentieth century. In the Roman Catholic pauper ground lies John Lynch, a Cork Fenian who died in Woking Prison in 1866, in a grave whose position is no longer known.

The Military Cemeteries

Commonwealth, American and Allied war graves

1917

In 1917 the company set aside land for servicemen and women who died in the London area, many of them of wounds in the capital’s hospitals. It became Brookwood Military Cemetery, cared for by what is now the Commonwealth War Graves Commission and usually described as the largest of its cemeteries in the United Kingdom. Covering some 37 acres, it holds 1,601 Commonwealth burials of the First World War and 3,476 of the Second, with almost 800 war graves of other nationalities. The Canadian section is particularly large, and includes 43 men who died of wounds after the Dieppe Raid of August 1942.

The American cemetery

Beside the Commonwealth war graves lies the Brookwood American Cemetery and Memorial, dedicated in 1937 and maintained by the American Battle Monuments Commission. It is the only American First World War cemetery in Britain. Most of its 468 dead, 41 of them unknown, died of disease, chiefly the Spanish influenza of 1918; most of the 564 missing named on the walls of its chapel were lost at sea. It is the only one of the Commission’s First World War sites where the missing outnumber the buried.

The 1939–1945 Memorial

The Brookwood 1939–1945 Memorial, designed by Ralph Hobday and inaugurated in the presence of Queen Elizabeth II on 25 October 1958, commemorates some 3,500 men and women of the Commonwealth forces who have no known grave. They include commandos killed in the Dieppe and St Nazaire raids and Special Operations Executive personnel who died in occupied Europe.

Horsell; the Chelsea Pensioners

In 1968, after vandalism, the Muslim soldiers buried in the burial ground on Horsell Common, near the Shah Jahan Mosque, were moved to the military cemetery; 24 of them lie in Plot 2A. The Royal Hospital Chelsea has buried its pensioners at Brookwood since 1893 or 1894, and since 1962 in a second plot behind the Canadian section of the military cemetery.

The Victoria Cross

Holders of the Victoria Cross lie elsewhere in the cemetery too: William Reynolds, a guardsman decorated for the Alma in 1854; Ross Lowis Mangles, one of the very few civilians ever to receive it, for carrying a wounded soldier for miles during the retreat from Arrah in 1857; John Sherwood-Kelly, decorated at Cambrai in 1917; and the chaplain William Addison, for tending the wounded under fire in Mesopotamia in 1916.

Chapter X.

Reading the Stones

The emblems of Victorian mourning

Victorian monuments were meant to be read. Carvers drew on a shared vocabulary of emblems, classical, Christian and sentimental, that many mourners of the day would have understood. Meanings were never fixed, and many stones mix several, but these are among the commonest.

The draped urn

A classical vessel for the ashes of the dead, used long after cremation had lapsed; the drape is commonly read as mourning, or as the veil between this life and the next.

The inverted torch

Life extinguished: a torch pointed downwards is a sign of death on Greek and Roman funerary monuments. Death carries one on the De Morgans’ stele.

The broken column

A life cut short: the unbroken column stands for a noble life, the broken one for an early death.

The anchor

Hope, from the Letter to the Hebrews, “hope we have as an anchor of the soul”; on some graves it may mark a sailor.

The winged hourglass

Time flies, and runs out for all. An old emblem of mortality that has graced gravestones for centuries.

Ivy

Evergreen and clinging: fidelity, friendship and undying memory.

The obelisk

An Egyptian form, sacred to the sun god Ra and commonly read as a sign of eternity; made fashionable in cemeteries by the Egyptian Revival.

The laurel wreath

Victory, here over death itself; often given to soldiers, scholars and artists.

The lily

Purity and resurrection; the white lily is the flower of the Virgin, and the Easter lily of the Resurrection.

Clasped hands

Commonly read as a bond between two people, a farewell, or a reunion in heaven. Cuffs can show which hand is the husband’s and which the wife’s.

    A note on method

    This guide was compiled in October 2026 from the published sources listed below. Every person, place, date and sentence of chapter text was then checked against the full text of those sources and corrected where they differed; statements that no readable source supports were removed. Where reliable accounts disagree, both readings are given, and each entry in the register and catalogue names its own sources and carries an evidence grade. A record of every check, with the passage relied on, is kept with the guide’s source files. Historic England’s and the Commonwealth War Graves Commission’s websites cannot be read automatically, so list entries were read in the copies published by British Listed Buildings, and war-grave figures were taken from the Brookwood Cemetery Society, the American Battle Monuments Commission and Wikipedia. Nothing here should be treated as a substitute for the cemetery’s own records.
