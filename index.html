Chapter I.

The Burial Question

London, 1840–1852

London’s churchyards full

By the 1840s London had run out of room for its dead. Its churchyards and small private burial grounds were full to the point of public scandal, and the new joint-stock garden cemeteries ringing the capital, Kensal Green, Highgate, Abney Park, Brompton and the rest, could not keep pace with a population that was doubling within a lifetime. Edwin Chadwick told Parliament that Spa Fields in Clerkenwell, a ground meant for perhaps 1,000 bodies, held some 80,000, and that gravediggers broke up older remains to make room for new. The figure was polemical, but no one seriously disputed the condition it described.

Cholera, 1848–49

Cholera turned a nuisance into an emergency. The epidemic of 1848–49 killed between 14,600 and 14,800 Londoners, and most sanitary reformers still believed that disease rose as a miasma from rotting matter, graveyards included. The General Board of Health’s Report on a General Scheme for Extramural Sepulture (1850) recommended ending burial inside the capital altogether.

The State tries, and withdraws

Parliament first tried to do the job itself. The Metropolitan Interments Act of 5 August 1850 made London a single burial district under the Board of Health, with power to close old grounds and open new national cemeteries. The scheme proved too costly and was never carried out. The Burial Act of 1 July 1852 repealed it and put a different mechanism in its place: individual burial grounds could now be closed by Order in Council, and parishes could form burial boards to provide new ones. Closures followed in quick succession, and the dead of central London needed somewhere to go.

Broun and Sprye’s scheme, 1849

Two promoters, Sir Richard Broun and Richard Sprye, had already seen the opportunity. In 1849 they proposed one vast cemetery on cheap heathland near Woking, some 37 km (23 miles) from London, served by its own funeral trains over the London and South Western Railway, which had reached Woking in 1838. A train could carry the dead further, faster and more cheaply than the slow horse-drawn hearses that served the existing cemeteries, and the distance answered the sanitary objection.

The founders bought out

The idea outlived its authors’ control of it. In April 1851 a group of trustees led by William Voules bought Broun and Sprye’s rights for £20,000 and formed a company on their own terms. Its private Act, An Act to incorporate the London Necropolis and National Mausoleum Company, received Royal Assent on 30 June 1852. The “National Mausoleum” of the title, a grand hall for the remains of the eminent, was never built.

Chapter II.

A City of the Dead at Woking

The land, the design and the opening, 1852–1890

The Onslow heath

Under its Act the company bought common land at Woking from Lord Onslow: 2,268 acres according to Historic England, a little over 2,000 in rounder accounts. It was poor, sandy heath, worth little for farming, and far more than a cemetery could ever use. The company laid out only some 350–500 acres at the western end, near the hamlet that would grow into Brookwood village; the rest became its land bank.

A boardroom in crisis

The company’s early life was quarrelsome. In early 1853, amid allegations of voting irregularities and with the promised dividends unpaid, several directors resigned, Voules among them, and public confidence in the scheme collapsed. Work on the ground nevertheless went ahead.

Smirke, Thomas and Donald

In 1853 Sydney Smirke, architect of the British Museum’s Reading Room, replaced the company’s first architect, Henry Abraham, and designed the two chapels and two stations inside the cemetery. Historic England credits the landscape to Smirke and William Broderick Thomas, laid out on the principles of J. C. Loudon, the great theorist of the garden cemetery, and the planting to Robert Donald of the Goldsworth Nursery at Woking, a friend of Loudon’s. The claim, sometimes repeated, that William Tite landscaped the grounds is poorly supported; Tite is better documented as architect of the London terminus.

Wellingtonias and rhododendrons

Donald’s planting is what visitors remember. Seed of the newly introduced American conifers was reaching Britain just as the cemetery opened, and Brookwood took them up with enthusiasm: giant sequoias, known in England as Wellingtonias, monkey puzzles and redwoods, set among rhododendrons planted to divide the plots and give each grave a measure of privacy. The mature Wellingtonias lining Long Avenue are the cemetery’s signature.

Church and Chapel divided

The ground was split by religion as well as by class. The southern part was consecrated for the Church of England; the northern part was left unconsecrated for Nonconformists and, as it turned out, for every other faith. Each half had its own chapel and its own railway station. That unconsecrated northern ground, required by law for dissenters, is the reason Brookwood was later able to welcome Parsis, Muslims and others long before most British cemeteries did.

7 and 13 November 1854

Charles Sumner, Bishop of Winchester, consecrated the southern half, then called the Woking Necropolis, on 7 November 1854. The first funeral train ran from London on 13 November, and the first burials followed the same day. The first entry in the register is the stillborn twin sons of Mr and Mrs Hore of Ewer Street, Borough, buried at the expense of the parish of St Saviour’s, Southwark. Almost all of the first month’s 43 burials were pauper funerals in unmarked graves. The first permanent memorial was raised to Sir Henry Goldfinch, who died on 21 November 1854.

Selling the Heath

Land sales keep the company afloat

The dead never came in the numbers the promoters had promised. They had spoken of 10,000 or more burials a year; Brookwood never handled more than about 4,500. The company survived by selling the land it did not need. In 1858 it sold 64 acres to the Government for the Woking Invalid Convict Prison. Around 150 acres went for the second Surrey County Asylum, later Brookwood Hospital. Ten acres on Maybury Common were sold for £750 to the Royal Dramatic College, a home for retired actors; when it failed, its buildings passed to Dr Gottlieb Leitner for his Oriental Institute, beside which the Shah Jahan Mosque was built in 1889. In 1878, through a third party so as to keep its distance, the company sold about an acre at St John’s to the Cremation Society, where Britain’s first crematorium carried out its first cremation on 26 March 1885. Woking Borough Council’s own history credits the company’s development of the heath in the 1850s with bringing about the “new” town of Woking around the station.

Chapter III.

Decline and Revival

1941 to the present day

After the railway

Without its railway the necropolis lost its reason for being so far from London, and the post-war decades were lean. Undergrowth and rhododendron spread over the older plots, buildings decayed, and the derelict South station burned in 1972. The London Necropolis Company was dissolved around 1975, and other private companies ran the cemetery for a decade. Its records survived only because a former employee rescued them; they are now at the Surrey History Centre.

Güney, 1985–2006

In 1985 Ramadan Güney bought the cemetery from its then owner, having first acquired burial rights for London’s Turkish Cypriot Muslims. He died in Cyprus on 2 November 2006 and is buried in one of the Muslim plots he helped to establish; his family ran the cemetery after him.

Woking Borough Council, 2014

In December 2014 Woking Borough Council bought the companies that owned the civilian cemetery, Brookwood Park Limited and Brookwood Cemetery Limited, on confidential terms. The war graves were never part of the sale. Brookwood has also been chosen as the reburial ground for remains excavated by HS2 from St James’s Gardens, Euston, an echo of the clearances of Victorian London.

Grade I; the Society

Historic England registered the cemetery at Grade I on the Register of Parks and Gardens on 25 January 1993, describing it as the largest cemetery in England, and many individual monuments are listed. It remains on the Heritage at Risk register, its condition “generally satisfactory but with significant localised problems”. The Brookwood Cemetery Society, a registered charity, leads walks, holds open days, publishes The Brookwood Express and has restored a long line of neglected graves, from Mangles’s in 2006 to Bradlaugh’s in 2021, when a new bust replaced the one stolen long before.

Chapter IV.

The Necropolis Railway

Westminster Bridge Road to Brookwood, 1854–1941

A private terminus at Waterloo

The railway was the whole premise of the scheme. The company built its own London terminus beside Waterloo, between Westminster Bridge Road and York Street (now Leake Street), designed by William Tite with Joseph Cubitt as engineer. Funeral trains ran from there over the London and South Western main line and turned off, just west of Brookwood, on to the company’s private branch into the cemetery.

121 Westminster Bridge Road, 1902

When the railway needed the site to enlarge Waterloo, it built the company a replacement across the road at 121 Westminster Bridge Road, which opened in 1902 (8 February, by the usual account). Its office block survives as Westminster Bridge House, and the first-class entrance can still be seen in the façade.

Class and creed, even in death

The trains kept the distinctions of Victorian society intact to the graveside. Coffins travelled on First, Second or Third Class tickets (“one-way, of course”, in the phrase of the railway’s historian, John Clarke), and mourners travelled in the class of funeral they had paid for. Third Class was almost always used for paupers buried at the expense of their parish. Anglicans and Nonconformists, living and dead, were kept apart all the way to their separate stations. Traffic

At first a train ran once a day, whenever funerals were booked. Sunday trains ended after October 1900, and by the 1930s it was unusual for one to run more than twice a week. Brookwood recorded 203,041 burials between 1854 and 1941, though not every coffin came by rail. The line also carried the contents of whole London burial grounds: in 1862 the building of Charing Cross station and its approaches cleared the Cure’s College ground in Southwark, and some 7,950 bodies went to Brookwood, the first of many such removals.

“Cold meat”

Railwaymen had their own names for the trains. John Clarke records “the stiffs” and “the dead meat train”; “cold-meat train” was genuine Victorian slang, applied in an 1874 newspaper to a train carrying bodies to “the Woking Cemetery”, though the period’s great slang dictionary insisted the phrase properly belonged to a late goods train to Aldershot. The popular “Stiffs Express” appears to be a modern coinage.

16–17 April 1941

The last funeral train left Westminster Bridge Road on 11 April 1941, reportedly carrying the Chelsea Pensioner Edward Irish. Five nights later, on 16–17 April, an air raid destroyed the company’s rolling stock and much of the terminus. After the war the directors judged the private train obsolete; from 1946 funerals travelled by ordinary Southern Railway services to Brookwood’s main-line station, and the track inside the cemetery was lifted around 1947–48. The North station was later demolished, and the derelict South station burned in 1972. Its platform survives beside the St Edward Brotherhood’s monastery, and the old trackbed is now Railway Avenue.

Plan of the Cemetery

With a key to the principal graves, monuments and grounds

The cemetery lies on the south side of the main line from Waterloo to Southampton, west of Brookwood station, and is crossed from north-east to south-west by the road called Cemetery Pales. The Nonconformist and later the faith grounds occupy the northern part; the Anglican cemetery, the military cemeteries and the St Edward Brotherhood lie to the south and west. Chapter VIII.

Parishes, Guilds and Faiths

The grounds within the ground

London parishes

The company sold land wholesale as well as grave by grave. London parishes bought grounds for their dead: St Saviour’s, Southwark, from the first day, and Bermondsey from 1854 until about 1900. Friendly societies, trades and charities followed, and the historian John Clarke has counted more than sixty organisations that used Brookwood.

Actors, Oddfellows, Commissionaires

In 1857 or 1858 the actor John Anson acquired about an acre for the Dramatic, Equestrian and Musical Sick Fund Association, the beginning of Actors’ Acre. The Oddfellows of the Metropolitan District reserved a plot in 1859 and raised a central memorial in 1861; the Ancient Order of Foresters followed in the 1860s. The Corps of Commissionaires, founded in 1859 to employ ex-servicemen, buried more than 580 of its members and their families here, two of them holders of the Victoria Cross. From 1893 the Royal Hospital Chelsea brought its in-pensioners to Brookwood.

The unconsecrated north

The northern ground, left unconsecrated for Nonconformists, proved hospitable to faiths the founders can hardly have imagined. It is the reason Brookwood holds one of the most varied collections of religious burial in Europe.

The Zoroastrian Ground

1862

London’s Parsi community opened its burial ground in the north-west of the cemetery in 1862, a year after it founded its religious trust fund; it is described as the only Zoroastrian burial ground in Europe. Its mausolea are among the most remarkable in the cemetery. That of Nowrosjee Nusserwanjee Wadia (d. 1899) is modelled on the tomb of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae. Three Tata mausolea stand in a row on a raised terrace, the first and central one built in 1904 for Jamsetji Tata, founder of the Tata group, with those of his sons Sir Ratanji and Sir Dorabji beside it. Two of the first Indian members of the House of Commons lie here too: Sir Mancherjee Bhownaggree, Conservative, and Shapurji Saklatvala, Communist.

The Muslim Grounds

1884: the first in Britain

In 1884 a plot of 27 feet by 24 feet was set aside for Dr Gottlieb Leitner’s Oriental Institute at Woking. Marked by a stone giving the direction of prayer, it is described as the first Muslim burial ground in Britain. The cemetery’s records counted 390 Muslim burials by 1949. In the twentieth century the converts and scholars of the Woking Muslim Mission were buried here: Lord Headley, the Irish peer who took the name Shaikh Rahmatullah al-Farooq; Marmaduke Pickthall and Abdullah Yusuf Ali, whose translations of the Qur’an are still read across the English-speaking world; and, in an unmarked grave, Abdullah Quilliam, founder of the Liverpool Muslim Institute.

Brookwood today

The Muslim presence has since become the cemetery’s mainstay. In 1983 the Turkish Cypriot businessman Ramadan Güney bought burial rights over 19 acres for his community, and in 1985 he bought the whole cemetery. In recent years more than seven in ten burials at Brookwood have been Muslim, in separate grounds for Sunni, Shia, Ismaili, Dawoodi Bohra, Ahmadiyya and other communities. Among them lie the Palestinian cartoonist Naji al-Ali, murdered in London in 1987, and the architect Dame Zaha Hadid, buried in 2016 between her father and her brother.

The Orthodox, the Swedes and the Catholics

St Edward the Martyr

Monks of the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia arrived on 18 March 1982 and founded the St Edward Brotherhood in the former Anglican chapels near the old South station. In September 1984 they enshrined relics identified as those of St Edward the Martyr, the Anglo-Saxon king murdered at Corfe in 978, after decades of dispute over who owned them.

Solander; a Fenian

The Swedish Church has its own ground, to which the coffin of Daniel Solander, the botanist who sailed with Banks and Cook in the Endeavour, was moved from Wapping in the early twentieth century. In the Roman Catholic pauper ground lies John Lynch, a Cork Fenian who died in Woking Prison in 1866, in a grave whose position is no longer known.

The Military Cemeteries

Commonwealth, American and Allied war graves

3,476Commonwealth burialsSecond World War

1917

In 1917 the company set aside land for servicemen and women who died in the London area, many of them of wounds in the capital’s hospitals. It became Brookwood Military Cemetery, cared for by what is now the Commonwealth War Graves Commission and usually described as the largest of its cemeteries in the United Kingdom. Covering some 37 acres, it holds 1,601 Commonwealth burials of the First World War and 3,476 of the Second, with almost 800 war graves of other nationalities. The largest Second World War group is Canadian, and includes 43 men who died of wounds after the Dieppe Raid of August 1942.

The American cemetery

Within the military cemetery lies the Brookwood American Cemetery and Memorial, dedicated in 1937 and maintained by the American Battle Monuments Commission. It is the only American First World War cemetery in Britain. Most of its 468 dead, 41 of them unknown, died of disease, chiefly influenza and the pneumonia that followed it; most of the 564 missing named on the walls of its chapel were lost at sea. It is the only one of the Commission’s First World War sites where the missing outnumber the buried.

The 1939–1945 Memorial

The Brookwood 1939–1945 Memorial, designed by Ralph Hobday and unveiled by Queen Elizabeth II on 25 October 1958, commemorates some 3,500 men and women of the Commonwealth land forces who have no known grave. They died in the Norwegian campaign of 1940, in raids on occupied Europe such as Dieppe and St Nazaire, or as special agents who died in captivity or with resistance movements.

Horsell; the Chelsea Pensioners

In 1968, after repeated vandalism, the Indian Army soldiers buried in the Muslim ground on Horsell Common, near the Shah Jahan Mosque, were moved to the military cemetery; 24 of them lie in Plot 2A. The Royal Hospital Chelsea has buried its pensioners at Brookwood since 1893, and since the early 1960s in a plot within the military cemetery.

The Victoria Cross

Holders of the Victoria Cross lie elsewhere in the cemetery too: William Reynolds, a guardsman decorated for the Alma in 1854; Ross Lowis Mangles, one of the very few civilians ever to receive it, for carrying a wounded soldier for miles during the retreat from Arrah in 1857; John Sherwood-Kelly, decorated at Cambrai in 1917; and the chaplain William Addison, for tending the wounded under fire in Mesopotamia in 1916.

Chapter X.

Reading the Stones

The emblems of Victorian mourning

Victorian monuments were meant to be read. Carvers drew on a shared vocabulary of emblems, classical, Christian and sentimental, that any mourner of the day would have understood. Meanings were never fixed, and many stones mix several, but the commonest at Brookwood are these.

The draped urn

A classical vessel for the ashes of the dead, used long after cremation had lapsed; the cloth is the veil between this life and the next.

The inverted torch

Life extinguished. A torch still burning while held downward suggests the soul lives on. Death carries one on the De Morgans’ stele.

The broken column

A life cut short, often that of a young man or the head of a family who died before his work was done.

The anchor

Hope, from St Paul’s “hope we have as an anchor of the soul”; on some graves it marks a sailor.

The winged hourglass

Time flies, and runs out for all. A favourite of earlier centuries, still carved by Victorian masons.

Ivy

Evergreen and clinging: fidelity, friendship and undying memory.

The obelisk

Egyptian eternity, made fashionable by the Egyptian Revival; cheaper than a mausoleum and visible from afar.

The laurel wreath

Victory, here over death itself; often given to soldiers, scholars and artists.

The lily

Purity and resurrection, the flower of Easter and of the Virgin; common on the graves of women and children.

Clasped hands

Farewell, or reunion in heaven. Cuffs often tell which hand is the husband’s and which the wife’s.

    Sources and Method

    A note on method

    This guide was compiled in October 2026 from published sources found by web search. Most pages could not be opened in full, so facts rest on the text of search results quoting those pages; where accounts differ, both readings are given, and each entry in the register and catalogue carries its own sources and an evidence grade. Dates of death, plot numbers and listing grades are as given by the sources cited. Positions on the plan are drawn from published coordinates where these exist and otherwise estimated from descriptions; they are marked accordingly. Nothing here should be treated as a substitute for the cemetery’s own records.

Each caption names the photographer or source and the licence.
